\section{Introduction}

On February 7, 2026, the Utah Jazz played the Orlando Magic in a National Basketball Association (NBA) game. The Jazz led 94-87 going into the fourth and final quarter. They then kept two of their best players, Lauri Markkanen and Jaren Jackson Jr., out of the game for the whole quarter. Orlando outscored them 33-23 and won 120-117~\cite{espn2026jazzmagic}. Five days later, the NBA fined the Jazz \$500,000 over that game and one other~\cite{nba2026fines}. The Jazz had company. The Ringer, a sports website, counted eight of the league's thirty teams trying to finish with the worst records that month~\cite{ringer2026tanking}. Losing on purpose in this way is called tanking.

Those teams wanted a better place in the draft, the yearly event in which NBA teams take turns choosing new players. Each turn is called a pick. Earlier picks give teams a better choice. In 2026, the available players were led by AJ Dybantsa and Darryn Peterson~\cite{ringer2026tanking}. Sixteen of the thirty teams reach the playoffs, the tournament that follows the regular season and decides the champion. The fourteen teams that miss the playoffs pick first in the draft. A lottery, which is a random drawing, decides which of them get the earliest picks. Under the lottery the NBA used from 2019 to 2026, the three teams with the worst win-loss records each had a 14\% chance at the first pick. The team with the fourteenth-worst record had a 0.5\% chance. The drawing decided only the first four picks. The rest went in reverse order of record. The worst remaining team picked next~\cite{nba2019lotteryreform}. A worse record therefore meant better odds in the drawing and an earlier pick. Taylor and Trogdon~\cite{taylor2002losing} compared three NBA seasons played under different draft rules. They found that teams lost more often when the rules rewarded losing.

The draft exists to help weak teams. That goal creates the problem. At this conference in 2020, Banchio and Munro~\cite{munro2020notanking} studied lotteries that set their odds by the end-of-season standings, the final ranking of teams by record. They proved that every such lottery gives some team a reason to lose on purpose, unless it gives all the teams that miss the playoffs the same odds. A lottery of this kind therefore cannot both favor the worst teams and remove the reason to lose. We call this the impossibility result. Every change the NBA has made to its lottery has traded one of these two goals against the other. The latest, called 3-2-1, was adopted in May 2026. It gives the three teams with the worst records fewer chances in the drawing than the seven teams just above them. The teams at the bottom therefore no longer gain by losing~\cite{oconnor2026lottery}. A team in the middle of the standings still can, because of the play-in tournament. The NBA is split into two conferences, East and West. In each one, teams that finish seventh to tenth enter the play-in, a short qualifying round for the last playoff spots. Under 3-2-1, the teams that finish ninth or tenth get more chances in the drawing than those that finish seventh or eighth. A team in that range therefore improves its chances by finishing lower~\cite{oconnor2026lottery}. The new lottery also sets its odds by the current season alone. A team that has been weak for many years therefore gets no extra help.

The impossibility result depends on measuring weakness by a single season's standings. We introduce Carry-Over Lottery Allocation (COLA). It measures weakness by playoff results over many seasons. Each team has a draft priority, a score that carries over from one season to the next. The carry-over gives the method its name. A team's priority grows each season it goes without playoff success. It falls when the team wins a playoff series or receives a top pick. A playoff series is one round of the playoffs between two teams. COLA works like a waiting list. One more loss this season leaves a team's place on the list unchanged. A draft that follows the list therefore need not reward losing. Help still reaches weak teams. The teams that stay weak are the ones that wait longest.

COLA is a family of rules for setting the draft order. We call each such rule a mechanism. Full-lottery COLA holds a drawing for every pick among teams that miss the playoffs. A team's chances are proportional to its priority. Simple COLA has no drawing. The team that has waited longest picks first. The NBA's 3-2-1 can be read as an extreme member of the family. This season's standings alone set its priority. It carries only one thing from year to year, a limit on how often the same team can win the top picks. The same priority could also be used in an auction, a different kind of draft in which teams bid for players or for draft positions. It would decide how much each team has to bid.

We focus on Simple COLA. For each team, it counts the seasons since the team last won a playoff series or received one of the first three draft picks. The team with the highest count picks first. If two teams have the same count, the one with more wins this season picks ahead of the other. When no team can improve its pick by losing on purpose, a mechanism is called incentive compatible. We prove that Simple COLA is incentive compatible. The proof rests on a few assumptions about how teams behave. All but one are standard. The exception is that a team would rather win a game than lose it to change which other teams it competes with for the top picks. Section 4 defends it.

We test ten mechanisms, including the NBA's old lottery and 3-2-1, in ZenGM Basketball-GM. It is a simulator whose code is public. It simulates every game and every change to each team's roster in realistic fictional leagues. Each mechanism runs on the same 30 leagues for 25 seasons. We compare the mechanisms on three measures. The first is how much they help the teams that have waited the longest. The second is how evenly playoff success spreads across teams over many seasons. We call it long-term parity. The third is the reward for losing, meaning how much a team's pick improves when it loses more games. Because every mechanism plays the same 30 leagues, we can compare any two of them league by league. For each comparison, we report a 95\% confidence interval, the range that very likely contains the true difference. The leagues are fictional. We therefore also check whether the reward for losing and the help for long-waiting teams look the same on real NBA standings, one season at a time.

Of the ten mechanisms, Simple COLA does the most for the teams that have waited longest. Under 3-2-1, the longest any team goes without a playoff series win or one of the first three picks averages 16.3 seasons. Under Simple COLA, it averages 5.5. No team that misses the playoffs ever gains an earlier pick in that season's draft by losing more games. The average wait for a team's next playoff series win is within about a tenth of a season of the wait under 3-2-1. Simple COLA has three costs. Sections 5 and 7 measure them. First, teams wait about 0.2 seasons longer to return to the playoffs. Second, a team that has waited a long time can gain by losing a first-round playoff series. A series win would send it to the back of the list. Third, the team with the third pick can sometimes gain over two drafts by losing enough games to pick fourth. A top-three pick resets a team's wait. The fourth pick does not. Every number in this paper can be reproduced with the code in our public repository (Appendix C). The 3-2-1 lottery expires after the 2029 draft unless the league votes to keep it~\cite{oconnor2026lottery}. The league will therefore soon choose a draft mechanism again.

\section{Background}

Teams have tanked more in the years when the draft paid more for losing. Until 1984, the last-place teams in both conferences flipped a coin for the first pick. The other teams picked in reverse order of record. For the next five drafts, a lottery gave every team that missed the playoffs the same chance at the first pick. We call this format equal odds. In 1990, the NBA weighted the lottery so a worse record brought a better chance~\cite{munro2020notanking}. Taylor and Trogdon~\cite{taylor2002losing} compared three seasons, one played under each format. They looked at teams that could no longer reach the playoffs. We call these eliminated teams. They found tanking by eliminated teams under reverse order and under the weighted lottery. They found none under equal odds. Price et al.~\cite{price2010tournament} extended the test to every game from 1977 to 2007. They found more late-season losing in the seasons when finishing last paid the most. Soebbing and Humphreys~\cite{soebbing2013gamblers} find that bookmakers set their odds as if eliminated teams tank. They find mixed evidence of tanking in the actual scores. Fornwagner~\cite{fornwagner2019incentives} finds that National Hockey League teams also lose more after elimination.

Banchio and Munro's impossibility result explains why equal odds were the exception~\cite{munro2020notanking}. Its proof needs only two teams. Take two eliminated teams with the same record that meet in the last game of the season. Suppose finishing lower gives a better chance at the first pick. The losing team then comes out ahead, so neither team wants to win. A lottery based on the final standings can favor the worst teams only by giving a lower finish a better chance. It therefore always creates this problem. Equal odds avoids the problem by favoring no team. The NBA abandoned equal odds after five drafts because owners felt it did not help the worst teams~\cite{soebbing2013gamblers}. The league's owners approved the 3-2-1 lottery 29 to 1 on May 28, 2026. It starts with the 2027 draft and moves back toward equal odds~\cite{bontemps2026lottery}. It expands the lottery from fourteen teams to sixteen. Each team gets one, two, or three chances in the drawing. The drawing decides all sixteen picks. The old lottery decided only the first four~\cite{oconnor2026lottery}.

Most other proposals still judge teams on a single season and change which of its games count. Banchio and Munro would update the lottery odds after every game. They would stop changing them when a team could first gain by losing~\cite{munro2020notanking}. We call that point the freeze. Applied to the 1988-89 season, their rule freezes the odds after the 332nd of 1,025 games. It gives last-place Miami a 45.5\% chance at the first pick. The odds then reflect how teams played before the freeze, regardless of what happened later. Charlotte finished with the second-worst record. Yet it would have had a 7.5\% chance. Indiana won eight more games than Charlotte and would have had an 18.5\% chance. More of Charlotte's losses came after the freeze. Kazachkov and Vardi~\cite{kazachkov2020tanking} would rank the teams that miss the playoffs by where they stood at a point in the season fixed in advance. Lenten et al.~\cite{lenten2018evaluating} would give the first pick to the team eliminated earliest. Gold~\cite{gold2010nhl} would give it to the team that wins the most games after it is eliminated. Banchio and Munro note that neither rule is fully incentive compatible~\cite{munro2020notanking}.

A last group drops the lottery. The wheel, designed by Mike Zarren of the Boston Celtics, fixes each team's draft position years in advance. Over thirty years, each team picks first once, second once, and so on down to thirtieth~\cite{lowe2013wheel,lowe2014wheel}. Losing buys nothing. A weak team also gets no extra help. Two other plans use an auction. Nate Silver~\cite{silver2026arc} would replace the draft with player bidding. Each team would get between 25 and 100 units to bid with each year. The worst records would get the most. A Boston Celtics proposal reported by Quinn~\cite{quinn2026credits} would have teams bid credits for draft positions. Unspent credits carry over to the next year. A team loses 20 credits for each playoff series it wins.

None of the draft proposals above look beyond the current season to decide which teams need help. The Celtics' credits do. They carry over and shrink when a team wins in the playoffs. Those credits work through an auction. COLA uses the same longer view to set the draft order directly, with no auction.

\section{The COLA framework}

\subsection{How a COLA mechanism works}

A COLA mechanism keeps one number for each team, called its priority. It updates that number once a year. After the playoffs, priority rises for the teams that went without success. In most mechanisms, success means a playoff series win. Priority falls for the teams that had success. The mechanism then sets the draft order from priority. The teams that receive the best picks give some of their priority back. The next season starts where this one ended. A long run without success therefore builds priority up. One good season or one high pick spends it.

Simple COLA shows this cycle in its plainest form. A team's priority is its wait, the number of seasons since it last won a playoff series or received one of the first three picks. A team whose last series win came six seasons ago, with no top-three pick since, has a wait of six. When the playoffs end, every team that won a series goes back to a wait of zero. Every other team adds one season. There are 22 such teams, the 14 that missed the playoffs and the 8 that lost in the first round. Those 22 teams pick first, in order of wait, longest first. If two teams have the same wait, the one with more wins this season picks first. The 8 teams that won a series pick after them. After the draft, the three teams that picked first go back to zero. The mechanisms in the family differ in three choices. We call these choices dials, because a league can set each one separately. The first dial is how priority grows. In every mechanism we study but one, each team that qualifies gets the same fixed step, whatever its record. A team that already qualifies therefore gains nothing more by losing more games. The exception is Capped COLA (Section 3.2). Its priority grows by a team's wins, so losing can cost a team priority. What a team might gain by losing its way into qualifying is the subject of Section 5. The second dial is how priority falls, meaning how much a playoff run or a high pick costs. The third dial is how priority becomes lottery odds and draft positions. It covers which teams take part, a group we call the pool. It also covers whether their order comes from a drawing, from priority alone, or from both.

\subsection{Seven COLA mechanisms}

Simple COLA is the first of the seven COLA mechanisms we test. Three others measure priority in lottery tickets. A ticket is one chance in a drawing. A team gains 1,000 tickets in each season without a playoff series win. Each series it wins takes away a quarter of the tickets it held at the start of the playoffs. A team with 5,000 tickets that wins two series keeps 2,500. A champion, with four series wins, keeps none. Picks cost tickets too. The first pick takes away all of a team's tickets. The second, third, and fourth picks take away 75\%, 50\%, and 25\%. In all three mechanisms, the pool is the 14 teams that missed the playoffs.

The three differ only in the third dial. Classic COLA draws the first four picks, giving each team a chance proportional to its tickets. It then hands out picks 5 to 14 in reverse order of record. Waitlist COLA draws the first four picks the same way. It hands out picks 5 to 14 in ticket order, with the team that has the most tickets going first. Full-lottery COLA draws all 14 picks, one after another, with each team's chance proportional to its tickets.

Two more mechanisms reward both winning and waiting. Countdown COLA uses Simple COLA's wait and its pool of 22 teams. It scores each team by its wait multiplied by its wins this season. Among teams with the same wait, the one that won more scores higher. It then fills the draft order from the last pick to the first. At each step, it draws one team from the five lowest scores not yet placed. Lower scores make a team more likely to be drawn. The teams with the best scores are placed last, so they pick first. Beckett COLA counts the seasons since a team last won a playoff series, finished in the top six of its conference, or received the first pick. Its pool is every team with a count of two or more. Each team gets entries equal to its count multiplied by its wins. The first four picks are drawn with chances proportional to entries. The rest of the pool picks in order of entries, most entries first.

The seventh, Capped COLA, was designed to limit what a team can gain by losing a playoff series on purpose (Section 5.1). It keeps a stockpile of tickets for each team. The six teams with the best records in each conference reach the playoffs directly, and their stockpiles do not grow. Every other team adds its regular-season wins to its stockpile, up to a cap of 150 tickets. A team gives tickets back as it succeeds. A team that comes through the play-in and loses in the first round gives up 10\%, and any other first-round loser gives up 20\%. A team that wins one, two, three, or four series gives up 40\%, 60\%, 80\%, or all of its tickets. The first five picks cost 100\%, 80\%, 60\%, 40\%, and 20\%.

Capped COLA's pool leaves out every team that won a series this season or last, or received a top-five pick last season. The first five picks are drawn with chances proportional to tickets. The rest of the pool picks in order of tickets, and then of wins. A team at the cap must keep its own first-round pick at least every other year. Otherwise it could trade its high picks away and never pay tickets for one. A team may also decline a pick that it does not think worth the tickets, for example in a weak draft class.

\subsection{Three reference lotteries}

We compare the seven COLA mechanisms with three lotteries that set priority from one season alone. The first is the lottery the NBA used from 2019 to 2026. We call it the old lottery. The second is an equal-odds lottery, in which all 14 picks among the teams that missed the playoffs are drawn with equal chances. The third is 3-2-1, as the NBA adopted it.

In the framework's terms, 3-2-1 rebuilds priority from this season's standings every year. Nothing grows with waiting. Success costs nothing in later years. The one thing it remembers is its limit on repeat picks. A team cannot receive the first pick two years in a row or a top-five pick three years in a row~\cite{oconnor2026lottery}. That places 3-2-1 at the edge of the family, with the first two dials set almost to zero. It also explains the two properties noted earlier. Its priority comes from one season's standings, so some teams can still gain by finishing lower. Nothing carries over, so a team that has been weak for years gets no extra help. Section 7 measures both.

\section{Incentive compatibility}

Section 1 called a mechanism incentive compatible when no team can improve its pick by losing on purpose. For Simple COLA, we can state that property exactly, prove it, and say what the proof assumes.

\subsection{The result}

Under Simple COLA, a game can change the pool order in two ways. First, it can change which teams win a first-round playoff series. Those eight teams leave the pool and return to a wait of zero. The list of series winners therefore fixes which teams are in the pool and how long each has waited. The second is the tie-break, the rule that separates teams with the same wait. It uses each team's regular-season win total. The pool order uses that total for nothing else. The theorem below addresses the second case. Appendix A proves it.

\textbf{Theorem 1.} Take any season under Simple COLA and any team that finishes it in the pool. Suppose the team loses a regular-season game it could have won. Suppose also that the same eight teams win a first-round series either way. Then the team's pick in that season's draft is the same as it would have been with the win, or later. It is never earlier.

Suppose two teams have each waited five seasons. One finishes with 30 wins and the other with 28. The 30-win team picks first of the two. Had it lost three more games, it would have finished with 27 wins and picked second. Its wait would still be five seasons. It would still pick after every team that had waited six seasons or more and before every team that had waited four or fewer.

\subsection{Why it holds}

The proof has three steps. First, the loss changes no team's wait. A wait counts the seasons since a team last won a playoff series or received a top-three pick. With the same series winners, every wait stays the same. Second, the loss changes two win totals. The team has one win fewer. Its opponent has one more. Third, the order compares wins only between teams with the same wait. The team with more wins picks first. Every team that would have picked ahead of the losing team still does. A team that was level with it or just behind may now pick ahead too. The number of teams picking before it can rise and cannot fall. A coin flip settles any tie on both wait and wins. A loss can therefore also turn a lead into a coin flip, or a coin flip into a place behind. The same steps apply one game at a time. They therefore cover any number of losses.

The impossibility result in Section 1 does not reach Simple COLA. That result covers lotteries that set odds based on final standings alone and give worse records odds at least as good as better ones. Simple COLA reads the standings in one place, the tie-break. There, it favors the team with more wins. Its help for weak teams comes from the wait. The wait rises by one season for every team in the pool, whatever its record. Simple COLA therefore does what the result rules out for a lottery based on the final standings. It favors the teams that have gone longest without success. It also gives no team in the pool an earlier pick for losing.

\subsection{What the result assumes}

Theorem 1 takes the list of series winners as given. Like the impossibility result, it looks at one season and its draft~\cite{munro2020notanking}. Three assumptions about how teams behave carry it to the claim that no team has a reason to lose a game.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A team prefers an earlier pick to a later one.
\item A team prefers winning a playoff series to going without one.
\item A team would rather win a game than lose it to change which other teams are in the pool.
\end{enumerate}

The first two are standard. In Banchio and Munro's model, teams value the first pick and a place in the playoffs at least as much~\cite{munro2020notanking}. Section 5 takes up the cases in which either assumption could fail and how a league can guard against them.

The third assumption needs a defense. A team's pick does depend on which other teams are in the pool. If a team that would have picked ahead leaves the pool, every team behind it moves up one place. One place is the most a team can gain from any one rival in this way.

Under Simple COLA, a rival leaves the pool only by winning a playoff series. Reaching the playoffs changes nothing in the draft. A team that loses in the first round stays in the pool. A team that wanted a rival out would have to help it win a series. The one kind of help that requires losing is a regular-season loss to that rival. That loss adds one win to the rival's total. In our simulated leagues, 14\% of teams that won 40 to 44 games won a playoff series. Among teams that won 45 to 49 games, 41\% did. That is the steepest rise in the data, about five percentage points for each extra win. It overstates what one handed-over win is worth. Stronger teams both win more games and win more series. A deliberate loss therefore buys at most about one-twentieth of a draft place. It buys that only when the rival is ahead in the order. The third assumption asks only that a team value a win more than that.

The loss also has costs inside the draft. It takes one win from the team's own total. In the simulated leagues, one more loss costs a team that missed the playoffs 0.15 places on average through the tie-break. The league also punishes such games, as the \$500,000 fine in Section 1 shows~\cite{nba2026fines}.

\subsection{A check on the simulated leagues}

A proof covers the rule as written. We also tested the rule as our simulator runs it. We first confirmed that the simulator's draft order followed the rule in all 750 seasons of the Simple COLA run, 25 seasons in each of 30 leagues. Then, in each season, we took every team that missed the playoffs except the one with the fewest wins. We lowered its win total one game at a time until it sat one win below that team. We held everything else constant. That gave 158,325 cases. Counting each coin-flip tie as half a place, the team's place in the order was later in 98,695 cases and unchanged in the other 59,630. It was earlier in none.

\section{When the assumptions fail}

Section 4 called two of its assumptions standard. This section takes each in turn and shows where it could fail. It measures the size of each failure in our simulated leagues and describes what a league could do about it. No team in the simulated leagues loses on purpose. Each number below therefore measures a temptation. It says nothing about how often a real team would act on it.

\subsection{A team that prefers the pool to the playoffs}

The second assumption says a team prefers winning a playoff series to going without one. It matters because every mechanism's pool ends at a line. A team just inside the pool picks earlier than a team just outside.

Under the old lottery, the line is the last playoff place. The best team that missed the playoffs picks 13.6th on average. The first pick outside the pool is the 15th. Staying in the pool is therefore worth 1.4 places. Under 3-2-1, the line is the play-in game between the seventh and eighth teams in a conference. The loser gets one chance in the drawing and picks 11.6th on average. The first pick outside the pool is 17th, a difference of 5.4 places. Under both lotteries, the team on the line holds the worst odds in the pool.

The difference is larger under COLA, because priority comes mostly from past seasons. The team on the line can have the highest priority. Under Full-lottery COLA, the team with the most tickets among those that missed the playoffs picks 5.4th on average, 9.6 places ahead of the first pick outside the pool. Under Simple COLA, the line is a first-round series. A playoff team that holds the longest wait picks first if it loses that series. It picks 23rd at best if it wins, a difference of 22 places. This is the second cost named in Section 1. Capped COLA, designed for this concern, halves Simple COLA's figure. Its pool is smaller, and its first five picks are drawn. Its team with the most tickets therefore picks 4.5th on average, 11.0 places ahead of the first pick outside its pool. A series win also keeps a team out of the next season's drawing.

To collect that difference, a team would have to lose a playoff series on purpose and give up the second round. A league that prefers not to rely on teams refusing that trade has two options. The first is already in our tests. A full-lottery COLA ends its pool at the last playoff place and draws every pick. The most a team can gain at the line is then 9.6 places. That gain comes only as better odds in a drawing. The second option is to count any playoff appearance as success. A first-round loss then buys nothing. That change has two costs. Help stops for a team that reaches the playoffs every year and never advances. The line also returns to the end of the regular season, where late losses can move a team across it. We have not simulated that variant.

\subsection{A team that prefers a later pick}

The first assumption says a team prefers an earlier pick to a later one. Under Simple COLA, it can fail at one line in the order, between the third and fourth picks. A top-three pick returns a team's wait to zero, a step we call a reset. The fourth pick leaves the wait untouched. Sometimes the teams on either side of that line have the same wait. Only wins then separate them. The team with fewer wins picks fourth and keeps its wait.

Every league starts with no history, so all its teams share one wait at first. We therefore measured this line in seasons 6 to 25 of each league. The third and fourth teams shared a wait in 410 of 600 seasons, or 68\%. In the following season, the team that had picked third picked 21st on average. The team that had picked fourth received a top-three pick 89\% of the time. To change places with the fourth team, the third team would have needed a median of six more losses.

The exception is narrow. It concerns the teams tied at one line, about four on average. It asks a team to trade the third pick now for the chance of a better one a year later. The simplest remedy is to break ties between teams with the same wait by a random draw. Wins then play no part in the order, and the exception disappears. The price is a small drawing and the loss of the tie-break's reward for winning. We have not simulated that variant. The ticket mechanisms avoid a single line in another way. They draw the first four picks and charge for them on a sliding scale. The first pick costs all of a team's tickets, and the fourth costs a quarter.

\subsection{The season after a top pick}

The reset behind that line is also what spreads the top picks among teams. Under the old lottery, 29\% of top-three picks went to a team that had received one in the previous two drafts. The share was 20\% under 3-2-1, 16\% under Full-lottery COLA, and 13\% under Capped COLA. Under Simple COLA, it was zero.

The cost falls on a team that takes a top-three pick and stays weak. Suppose it missed the playoffs again the next season. Its pick that season averaged 21.0 under Simple COLA and 20.4 under Capped COLA, which keeps the team out of the next drawing. It averaged 6.6 under the old lottery, 8.6 under 3-2-1 and 9.4 under Full-lottery COLA. Such a team then waits. On average, 7.6 seasons pass between one team's top-three picks under Simple COLA and 6.3 under Capped COLA, against 4.9 under the old lottery. Section 7 measures how this affects the time teams take to return to the playoffs. This wait is the price of helping the teams that have waited longer. A milder reset would return the team to the top of the draft sooner, ahead of them. A league that still wants a shorter wait after a top pick can soften the reset, as the ticket mechanisms do with their sliding scale.

\subsection{Draft classes}

No mechanism we test looks at how strong a draft class is. Under Simple COLA, a team's turn at the front comes when its wait says so, in a strong year or a weak one. A lottery leaves the same question to chance. Capped COLA lets a team decline a pick it does not want, which keeps its tickets for a stronger class. Our simulated teams never decline a pick, so the runs do not measure this option.

\subsection{What a remedy costs}

Each remedy in this section adds a rule to a mechanism whose appeal is that it has so few. Simple COLA keeps one count for each team and holds no drawing. A tie-break by random draw adds a small drawing. Full-lottery COLA adds tickets, percentages, and a drawing for every pick. Capped COLA adds a cap, a schedule of reductions, a rule on trading picks, and the option to decline a pick. The choice depends on how much weight a league gives to the exceptions above. Sections 7 and 8 return to it by comparing all ten mechanisms.

\section{Simulation design}

A proof covers one season's draft. Help for the teams that wait and long-term parity show only over many seasons. We therefore test the ten mechanisms in simulated leagues. This section describes the simulator, what we ran, what we measured, and how the simulated leagues compare with the NBA.

\subsection{The simulator}

ZenGM Basketball-GM is a basketball management game with public code~\cite{zengm}. Its user acts as a team's general manager. A general manager decides which players the team drafts, signs, and trades.

The simulator creates a league of fictional players. Each player has ratings, numbers that describe the player's skills. The simulator plays every game one possession at a time. A possession is one team's turn with the ball. What happens on each possession depends on the ratings of the ten players on the court.

The simulator also runs everything around the games. Players improve, age, get injured, and retire. Teams sign players to contracts under a salary cap, a limit on what a team pays its players in total. Teams also trade players and compete for free agents, the players whose contracts have ended. Each year, a new group of prospects enters through a draft of two rounds. Prospects are the young players available to be drafted. Each team starts with one pick in each round. The simulator's built-in general managers run all 30 teams, so no person makes a decision in any league.

A league has the shape of the NBA. Thirty teams in two conferences play 82 games each. A play-in and playoffs for 16 teams follow. Each playoff series goes to the first team to win four games. Draft order matters in these leagues for the same reason it matters in the NBA. The simulated teams strongly favor the best prospects still available. A team's results come from the players it has.

The simulator's developer added COLA in February 2026 as a draft format users can choose~\cite{zengm2026cola}. In this study, our own code sets the first-round draft order under each of the ten mechanisms. The simulator does everything else. It has no rule that tells a team to lose, so no simulated team loses on purpose. Where we report a reward for losing, we compute what a team would have gained by losing. No team in the leagues acts on it.

\subsection{What we ran}

We ran each of the ten mechanisms on 30 leagues for 25 seasons. That makes 7,500 simulated seasons and about 9.2 million regular-season games.

A simulated league depends on chance at every step, from the players it starts with to each shot and each lottery drawing. One number, called a random seed, fixes all of those chance events. A given seed and mechanism therefore always produce the same league. We used the same 30 seeds for every mechanism. A league starts with the same players under all ten mechanisms and plays an identical first season. The ten versions of a league begin to separate at the first draft. By the fourth season, no two of them are alike.

Every league starts with no history, so every wait and every ticket count begins at zero. One approximation applies to 3-2-1. Its pool includes the loser of each seven-eight play-in game. In our simulated leagues, the team with the eighth-best record in each conference stands in for that loser. Capped COLA leaves three things to a simulation. They are where the teams outside its pool pick, whether a team declines a pick, and how its rule on trading picks applies. Appendix B gives our choices and the remaining details of each mechanism as we ran it.

\subsection{What we measured}

Section 1 listed three things we measure: help for the teams that have waited longest, long-term parity, and the reward for losing. This subsection makes them exact. It defines one number for the reward for losing and two each for help and parity.

Let $p_i$ be the number of the pick that team $i$ receives in a season's draft, so $p_i = 1$ means the first pick. Under a mechanism with a drawing, $p_i$ depends on chance. We then use its expected value $\mathrm{E}[p_i]$, the average pick over all the ways the drawing could come out. A team with an equal chance at the first pick and the third pick has an expected pick of 2. We compute each expected value exactly. We go through every possible outcome of the drawing and its probability, so the value carries no random error.

\textbf{The reward for losing.} Take one season and the team with the fifth-worst record among the 14 that missed the playoffs. Call it team $m$. Suppose it had finished with the worst record in the league and nothing else had changed. Every other team keeps its record, and every team keeps its priority. Under Capped COLA, the worse record also takes from team $m$ the tickets its lost wins would have added. Write $\mathrm{E}[p_m^{\mathrm{worst}}]$ for team $m$'s expected pick in that case. The reward for losing is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
R = \mathrm{E}[p_m] - \mathrm{E}[p_m^{\mathrm{worst}}].
\end{equation}
Suppose the team would pick sixth on average as things stand and fifth on average with the worst record. Then $R$ is +1. A positive $R$ means the worse record brought an earlier pick. A zero or negative $R$ means losing bought nothing or cost the team places. The fifth-worst record is our standard starting point. Section 7 reports the others.

\textbf{The help for a longer wait.} Take the team with the fifth-highest priority among the same 14 teams. Call it team $q$. Suppose its priority alone had been one step above the highest in its pool. A step is one season of waiting or 1,000 tickets. Under Capped COLA, a step is the team's own wins that season, up to the cap of 150 tickets. Write $\mathrm{E}[p_q^{\mathrm{top}}]$ for team $q$'s expected pick in that case. The help for a longer wait is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:help}
H = \mathrm{E}[p_q] - \mathrm{E}[p_q^{\mathrm{top}}].
\end{equation}
It counts the draft places a mechanism gives a team for having waited the longest. Under a lottery in which nothing carries over, $H$ is zero.

\textbf{The longest wait.} For each team, we count the seasons in a row in which it neither won a playoff series nor received a top-three pick. Write $w_i(s)$ for team $i$'s count after season $s$ and its draft, with $w_i(0) = 0$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
w_i(s) =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if team $i$ won a series or received a top-three pick in season $s$,} \\
w_i(s-1) + 1 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is Simple COLA's wait, counted the same way under every mechanism. The longest wait in a league is the largest count any team reaches in any season,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:longest}
L = \max_{i,\,s} \, w_i(s).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Two waits for playoff success.} A stretch of seasons without a series win ends when the team wins a series. Suppose a team wins a series, goes four seasons without one, and then wins again. It has completed a stretch of four seasons. The wait for the next series win is the average length of the stretches that ended during a league's 25 seasons. The wait to return to the playoffs is the same average for stretches of seasons outside the playoffs. A stretch still open when the league ends is left out, under every mechanism alike. Shorter waits mean that a team that falls behind recovers sooner. These two numbers therefore measure long-term parity.

Each measure gives one number per league. $R$ and $H$ are averaged over the league's 25 seasons. The other three are computed once per league. We report the average over the 30 leagues.

\subsection{How we compare two mechanisms}

To compare mechanisms $A$ and $B$ on a measure $X$, we take the difference in each league, $d_\ell = X^A_\ell - X^B_\ell$ for leagues $\ell = 1, \dots, 30$. Comparing two mechanisms league by league in this way is called pairing. We report the average difference $\bar{d}$ with a 95\% confidence interval,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval}
\bar{d} \;\pm\; t \, \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{30}},
\end{equation}
where $s_d$ is the standard deviation of the 30 differences, a measure of how far apart they lie. The multiplier $t = 2.045$ is the standard one for a 95\% interval built from 30 values (the 97.5th percentile of Student's $t$ distribution with 29 degrees of freedom). When the interval excludes zero, we say the two mechanisms differ on that measure.

Each league counts once, because the seasons of one league depend on each other. Pairing guarantees that both mechanisms start from the same 30 leagues. Its effect fades after the first draft. Across the 30 leagues, a league's longest wait under one mechanism is almost unrelated to its longest wait under another. The correlation measures how closely two quantities move together. It runs from −1 to 1, and 0 means they are unrelated. For the longest wait, it averages −0.02 over the 45 pairs that ten mechanisms make. For the two average waits, it averages 0.01 each. The intervals are therefore about as wide as intervals from unrelated leagues would be.

\subsection{The other seven mechanisms}

Table~\ref{tab:results} also reports the equal-odds lottery and six more COLA mechanisms. The equal-odds lottery favors no team. It gives no reward for losing and no help for a longer wait. Its longest wait averaged 16.0 seasons, 0.2 fewer than under 3-2-1 (−1.6 to 2.1).

Against 3-2-1, every COLA mechanism shortened the longest wait on average (Figure~\ref{fig:longest}). Classic, Waitlist, Full-lottery, Countdown, and Beckett COLA shortened it by 2.2 to 6.9 seasons, and each of their intervals excludes zero. Capped COLA shortened it by 1.8 seasons, and its interval includes zero (−0.2 to 3.8). Four of the six, Waitlist COLA, Full-lottery COLA, Countdown COLA, and Capped COLA, also gave no reward for losing from any position in any season. On long-term parity, Capped COLA was level with 3-2-1. The intervals for both of its waits include zero.

Simple COLA is therefore one of several COLA mechanisms that remove the reward for losing and help the teams that wait. Of the ten mechanisms, it gives the most help. It is also the only one that holds no drawing. Section 8 turns to how a league might choose among them.

\section{Discussion and adoption}

\subsection{Choosing a mechanism}

Figure~\ref{fig:map} places the ten mechanisms on the two sides of the dilemma in Section 1, the reward for losing and the longest wait. The three lotteries sit at the top, with longest waits of 16.0 to 16.4 seasons. All seven COLA mechanisms sit lower. Simple COLA sits lowest, at 5.5 seasons.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/mechanism-map}
\caption{The ten mechanisms on the reward for losing from the fifth-worst record and on the longest wait, both from Table~\ref{tab:results}. A point to the left of the dashed line means that dropping to the worst record costs a team places. A lower point means a shorter longest wait.}
\label{fig:map}
\end{figure}

Simple COLA suits a league that wants the most help for the teams that wait and the simplest rule. Its whole rule is short. Teams without a playoff series win pick first, ordered by the most seasons since their last series win or top-three pick. Ties go to the team with more wins. It holds no drawing, so the order is known as soon as the first round of the playoffs ends. Its costs are the two exceptions of Section 5. Its teams also return to the playoffs 0.18 seasons more slowly than under 3-2-1 (Section 7.3).

Full-lottery COLA suits a league that gives those exceptions more weight. It also suits a league that wants to keep a lottery night, the televised drawing that settles the top of the order. It draws every pick in proportion to tickets. Its pool ends at the last playoff place, so a team can gain at most 9.6 places at the line (Section 5.1). In our leagues, it gave no reward for losing from any position in any season. It shortened the longest wait by 4.3 seasons against 3-2-1 (2.9 to 5.8). Its teams also returned to the playoffs as quickly as under 3-2-1. The difference was −0.06 seasons (−0.17 to 0.06).

Capped COLA suits a league that wants every team outside its conference's top six to gain from each win. Its tickets grow with wins, so in our leagues losing never gained a team places, from any position in any season. A team at its line can gain 11.0 places, half of Simple COLA's 22.0 (Section 5.1). The price is less help for the teams that wait. Its longest wait, 14.5 seasons, is the longest of the seven COLA mechanisms and 1.8 seasons shorter than under 3-2-1 (−0.2 to 3.8).

The NBA has already moved in this direction. Its 3-2-1 lottery removed the reward for losing at the bottom of the standings. It also added a memory of recent picks (Section 3.3). Letting priority carry over is the next step. A league can take that step in any season. Every team's wait and tickets can be read from past seasons, as the next subsection does for 2026.

\subsection{Auction drafts}

Section 2 described two plans that use an auction. An auction still needs a rule for how much each team has to bid. That rule can reward losing, just as a draft order can. Silver's plan gives the most units to the worst records~\cite{silver2026arc}. A worse record therefore still buys a team more to spend. The Celtics' credits already carry over and shrink with playoff success~\cite{quinn2026credits}. A COLA priority could set the amounts in either plan. A team's budget would then grow while it waits, and a worse record this season would add nothing.

\subsection{What one simulator can show}

All of our comparisons come from one simulator. Every mechanism ran on the same 30 leagues. The simulator therefore shows how the mechanisms compare when everything else is equal. On the NBA's real standings, the reward for losing and the help for a longer wait came out as they did in the simulated leagues (Figure~\ref{fig:realcheck}). The simulator shows the size of each effect less exactly. Its teams stay strong or weak for longer than NBA teams do (Section 6.5). Every mechanism's longest wait would therefore likely be shorter in the NBA.

\section{Conclusion}

For decades, the NBA has traded help for its weakest teams against the tanking that this help invites. The tradeoff came from measuring weakness by one season's record. COLA measures weakness by how long a team has gone without playoff success. Priority builds while a team waits, and success spends it. Under the assumptions of Section 4, losing more games never earns a team an earlier pick under Simple COLA in that season's draft.

Simple COLA is the plainest member of the family. It orders the teams without a series win by how long they have waited. It holds no drawing. In 30 simulated leagues, it gave no reward for losing from any position. It cut the longest wait from 16.3 seasons under 3-2-1 to 5.5. It kept the wait for the next series win level with the old lottery and 3-2-1. Sections 5 and 7 measure its costs. One is a return to the playoffs 0.18 seasons slower than under 3-2-1.

The wider family lets a league choose its own balance of targeted help, parity, simplicity, and robustness. The NBA's 3-2-1 already sits at its edge. Helping the weakest teams and ending tanking can go together once weakness is measured over many seasons.

\subsection*{A. Proof of Theorem 1}

Take a season under Simple COLA. Let $P$ be its pool, the 22 teams that did not win a first-round series. For a team $j$ in $P$, write $w_j$ for its wait and $v_j$ for its regular-season wins. A coin flip settles any tie on both. We treat the coin flips as one random order of the teams, drawn independently of the game results. We write $j \succ i$ when team $j$ comes before team $i$ in that order. Simple COLA places $j$ ahead of $i$ when $w_j > w_i$, when $w_j = w_i$ and $v_j > v_i$, or when $w_j = w_i$, $v_j = v_i$, and $j \succ i$. The pool takes the first 22 picks. A team's pick is therefore one more than the number of teams in $P$ placed ahead of it.

Compare two outcomes of one regular-season game between a team $i$ in $P$ and an opponent $o$. In the first, $i$ wins. In the second, $i$ loses. The same eight teams win a first-round series in both. The random order is also the same. Primes mark the values after the loss.

The pool is the same in both outcomes, because it holds every team without a series win. Every wait is also the same. A wait counts the seasons since a team last won a playoff series or received a top-three pick. Neither this season's series winners nor any earlier pick changes. So $w'_j = w_j$ for every team $j$. The loss takes one win from $i$ and gives one to $o$. So $v'_i = v_i - 1$, and $v'_j \ge v_j$ for every other team $j$.

Now take any team $j$ that is placed ahead of $i$ after the win. If $w_j > w_i$, then $w'_j > w'_i$. If $w_j = w_i$ and $v_j > v_i$, then $v'_j \ge v_j > v_i > v'_i$. If $w_j = w_i$, $v_j = v_i$, and $j \succ i$, then $v'_j \ge v_j = v_i > v'_i$. In each case, $j$ is still placed ahead of $i$ after the loss. The teams ahead of $i$ after the win therefore stay ahead after the loss. So $i$'s pick is the same or later. Repeating the argument one game at a time covers any number of losses.

The proof covers one draft. Section 5.2 takes up the next draft, in which a top-three pick resets a wait.

\subsection*{B. The rules as run}

Our driver sets the first-round order under each mechanism. The simulator sets the second round as it normally does. A mechanism fixes where each team's own first-round pick falls. A traded pick keeps that place, and the team that holds it makes the selection. Every drawing uses the league's random seed. Every league starts with all tickets, waits, and counts at zero. Where a rule below leaves two teams level, they keep a fixed order. Under Simple COLA, a random order drawn from the seed settles them.

Classic, Waitlist, and Full-lottery COLA share one ticket rule. After the playoffs, a team without a series win gains 1,000 tickets. A team that won one, two, three, or four series keeps 75\%, 50\%, 25\%, or none of its tickets. After the draft, the teams with picks 1 to 4 keep none, 25\%, 50\%, and 75\% of theirs. Every result is rounded to a whole ticket.

\textbf{Old lottery.} The pool is the 14 teams that missed the playoffs, ranked by record, worst first. The first four picks are drawn without replacement, with 140, 140, 140, 125, 105, 90, 75, 60, 45, 30, 20, 15, 10, and 5 chances in 1,000 by rank. Picks 5 to 14 go to the rest of the pool by record, worst first. The 16 playoff teams follow, worst record first. Teams with the same record keep a fixed order. The NBA would split their odds.

\textbf{Equal-odds lottery.} The pool is the same 14 teams. All 14 picks are drawn without replacement, with equal chances. The playoff teams follow, worst record first.

\textbf{3-2-1.} The pool is the eight worst records in each conference, 16 teams in all. In each conference, the five worst are the teams that miss the play-in. The next two are the ninth and tenth seeds. The eighth-worst stands in for the loser of the seven-eight game. The three worst records in the league hold 2 chances each. The other seven teams that miss the play-in hold 3. The ninth and tenth seeds hold 2, and the two stand-ins hold 1. All 16 picks are drawn without replacement under three limits. No team receives the first pick in two straight drafts. No team receives a top-five pick in three straight drafts. The three worst records are placed by pick 12. Once the picks left through 12 equal the number of those teams still undrawn, the next picks are drawn among them. A limit that would leave no team to draw is skipped. The other 14 teams follow, worst record first.

\textbf{Classic COLA.} The pool is the 14 teams that missed the playoffs. The first four picks are drawn without replacement. Each team's chance is proportional to its tickets, or equal if no team left has any. Picks 5 to 14 go to the rest of the pool by record, worst first. The playoff teams follow, worst record first.

\textbf{Waitlist COLA.} The rules are those of Classic COLA, except that picks 5 to 14 go by tickets, most first.

\textbf{Full-lottery COLA.} The pool is the same 14 teams. All 14 picks are drawn without replacement. Each team's chance is proportional to its tickets, or equal among the teams left if none has any. The playoff teams follow, worst record first.

\textbf{Simple COLA.} After the playoffs, a team that won a series returns to a wait of zero. Every other team's wait grows by one. After the draft, the teams with picks 1 to 3 return to zero. The pool is the 22 teams without a series win. It picks in order of wait, longest first, and then by more wins. A random order drawn from the seed settles any tie that remains. The eight series winners follow, shortest playoff run first and then fewer wins first.

\textbf{Countdown COLA.} The pool and the wait are those of Simple COLA. A team's score is its wait times its wins. The pool is ranked by score, then wait, then wins, highest first. The order is filled from pick 22 up. Each step draws one of the five lowest-ranked teams left, with 6, 5, 4, 3, and 2 chances from the lowest up. The last team left takes the first pick. The series winners follow as under Simple COLA.

\textbf{Beckett COLA.} After the playoffs, a team's count returns to zero if it won a series or finished in the top six of its conference by wins. Otherwise, it grows by one. After the draft, the team with the first pick returns to zero. The pool is every team with a count of two or more. A team's entries are its count times its wins. The first four picks are drawn without replacement in proportion to entries. The rest of the pool follows by entries, most first. The teams outside the pool follow, shortest playoff run first and then fewer wins first. A team that missed the playoffs counts as having the shortest run.

\textbf{Capped COLA.} After the regular season, every team outside the top six seeds of its conference adds its wins to its stockpile, up to 150 tickets. The seeds come from the simulator's playoff bracket. A team that reached the playoffs through the play-in and lost in the first round keeps 90\% of its tickets, and any other first-round loser keeps 80\%. These reductions come before the drawing, which Capped COLA holds after the first round. The pool is every team without a series win this season or last and without a top-five pick last season. In a league's first season, no team sits out for the season before. The first five picks are drawn without replacement, each in proportion to tickets. A team without tickets is not drawn, and if no team left holds any, the drawing ends. The rest of the pool follows by tickets, most first, and then by wins. After the draft, the teams with picks 1 to 5 keep none, 20\%, 40\%, 60\%, and 80\% of their tickets. A team that won one, two, three, or four series keeps 60\%, 40\%, 20\%, or none. Stockpiles are rounded to the cent after each step. Three choices are ours. The teams outside the pool follow it by tickets and then wins, the order Capped COLA gives picks 6 and later. No team declines a pick, because the simulator has no rule for that choice. Capped COLA requires a team at the cap to hold its own first-round pick at least every other year. The simulator cannot enforce that rule. A top-five pick's cost and the next season's exclusion therefore fall on the team whose own pick it was. The other ticket mechanisms charge their costs the same way. In our runs, another team held 648 of the 3,750 top-five picks, 17\%, on lottery day.
